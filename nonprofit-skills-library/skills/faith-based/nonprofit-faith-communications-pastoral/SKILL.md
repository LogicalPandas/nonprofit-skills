# Faith-Based Nonprofit Pastoral Communications

## Boundaries

This skill is the pastoral (shepherd) voice. Route the rest:

- Executive-register messages from the ED seat, even at a faith-based org (all-staff, funder
  updates, town halls, layoffs, board-facing statements) → `nonprofit-executive-communications`.
- Brand voice, taglines, marketing copy → `nonprofit-brand-messaging`.
- Press releases and reporters during a misconduct crisis → `nonprofit-media-relations`; this skill
  writes the coordinated but separate letter to the congregation.
- Giving letters, pledge campaigns, contribution statements → `nonprofit-faith-stewardship-giving`.
- Drafting, debating and adopting a denominational statement → `nonprofit-faith-church-governance`;
  this skill writes the letter that carries the adopted decision.
- Mandatory-reporter rules and clergy-penitent privilege → `nonprofit-faith-employment-ministerial-exception`
  (touch them here only to route out).

## Core Frameworks

Name the frame before drafting; a message written against the wrong frame reads as tone deaf even
when every sentence is technically correct.

- **Pastoral voice vs. brand vs. executive vs. media voice.** Four distinct registers.
  - **Pastoral voice**: first-person singular ("I write to you today…"), shepherd stance,
    presence over performance, meaning-making rather than positioning, lament permitted, care for
    the reader over control of the narrative. Signed by clergy or by an ED explicitly stepping
    into a pastoral role. Silence and brevity are often more pastoral than volume.
  - **Brand voice**: third-person org identity ("[Org] has always stood for…"), controlled by
    marketing/communications, aimed at differentiation, uses tagline language, avoids first-person
    grief. Wrong register for a death or a tragedy.
  - **Executive voice**: first-person plural ("Our team learned yesterday…"), signed by the ED,
    optimized for staff/board/funder clarity, names decisions and next steps. Right for a layoff
    or a strategic pivot; wrong for a memorial.
  - **Media voice**: third-person, quotable, on-the-record, drafted with counsel, aimed at
    reporters. A pastoral letter is not a press release; when both are needed for the same event,
    draft them separately and coordinate the facts.
- **The "should we say anything" test.** Five questions, run in the checklist below. Silence
  about a local shooting reads as absence; silence about a distant partisan controversy may read
  as restraint.
- **Prophetic speech vs. partisan endorsement.** Naming public wrongs (poverty, violence,
  injustice) in the tradition's own moral vocabulary is prophetic speech and squarely inside
  501(c)(3) territory. Endorsing or opposing a candidate for public office, coordinating with a
  campaign, or rating candidates on a scorecard tied to an election crosses the **Johnson Amendment** (IRC §501(c)(3)
  absolute prohibition on political-campaign intervention) and can jeopardize exempt status. Near
  an election, route named-issue statements to a lawyer or to `nonprofit-faith-church-governance`
  for the political-activity discipline before publishing.
- **Calendars shift; verify every date.** Eastern Orthodox Pascha often follows the Julian
  calendar; Jewish (lunisolar) and Islamic (lunar, about 11 days earlier each Gregorian year) dates
  move every secular year. Observance of Shabbat and holy days varies across Reform, Conservative,
  Orthodox and Reconstructionist communities — a Yom Kippur email that lands Friday evening will
  not be read until Sunday in many. Check moonsighting sources (or the community's convention)
  before fixing Ramadan or Eid dates. Seasonal cadence is under "Liturgical / Annual Calendar Cadence".
- **Denominational vs. congregational locus of authority.** Local clergy speak for the
  congregation; a bishop, presbytery, conference minister, denominational executive, or head of
  communion speaks for the wider body. On some matters (a local death) only the local voice can
  speak; on others (a policy of the tradition) only the denominational voice can bind; on many the
  two must be aligned in advance (through `nonprofit-faith-church-governance`) so members don't
  receive conflicting messages the same weekend.

## Standard Deliverables

Pick the artifact deliberately — the wrong artifact for the moment is the most common failure.

- **Funeral / memorial / death notice** — clergy to congregation and family; ED to staff on the
  death of a colleague, board member, or major stakeholder.
- **Wedding, baptism, bar/bat mitzvah, aqiqah, or life-milestone announcement** — in the
  tradition's typical framing, with consent from the family.
- **Illness or hospitalization notice** — only what the person or family has authorized.
- **Crisis and tragedy response** — local violence, mass-casualty events, natural disasters,
  national moments (elections, mass shootings, hate crimes, verdicts).
- **"Should we say anything" pastoral decision memo** — the short internal note that resolves
  whether and how clergy speaks on a given moment.
- **Clergy misconduct disclosure to the congregation** — coordinated with legal, insurance, and
  denominational counsel; drafted alongside (never in place of) the press response.
- **Denominational statement / bishop's letter / pastoral letter** — from a bishop, denominational
  executive, or head of communion on a theological, moral, or policy matter.
- **Farewell letter (outgoing clergy) and arrival letter (incoming clergy)** — the pastoral
  bookends of a clergy transition.
- **Sermon-adjacent weekly congregation email** — extends the Sabbath/Sunday/Friday message into
  the week.
- **Prayer request / prayer-chain content** — with confidentiality discipline about what may be
  shared, and by whom.
- **Liturgical-season greeting or letter** — Advent, Lenten, Easter, High Holy Days, Ramadan,
  Eid, or the org's chosen cadence pegged to its constituents' traditions.
- **Building or institutional milestone** — dedication, cornerstone laying, mortgage-burning,
  deconsecration, closure of a congregation, merger with another congregation.

## The "Should We Say Anything" Pastoral Decision Checklist

Run before drafting when the trigger is a public moment (a shooting, a hurricane, an election
result, a verdict, a denominational controversy, a national tragedy) — not for routine pastoral
events (a member's death, a wedding, a scheduled holy day), which already require a word.

1. **Name the event in one sentence, and the community that will read the message.** "A mass
   shooting at a synagogue 2,000 miles away; our congregation is a Reform synagogue in a city with
   an active Jewish federation." Specificity prevents a generic message that lands nowhere.
2. **Answer the five questions.** (a) Does this community expect a word from clergy on this? (b)
   Does our tradition typically speak on matters of this kind? (c) Is silence itself a message
   here? (d) Who is uniquely served by hearing from us? (e) What will we regret saying, or not
   saying, in six months? Write one honest sentence for each. If four of five point toward
   speaking, speak; if two or fewer, hold; if three, escalate to the clergy for a judgment call.
   Silence is a decision, not a default; when the community is directly touched (its own children
   were at the school), default to speaking. Answered honestly, the test often says "not our word
   this time" — an org that speaks on everything loses the pastoral voice's weight.
3. **Distinguish prophetic speech from partisan endorsement.** If the moment is inside 60 days of
   a federal election and the message would name a candidate, party, or scorecard, stop and route
   to counsel and `nonprofit-faith-church-governance` for Johnson Amendment analysis before continuing.
4. **Pick the channel.** Pulpit-only, pulpit-plus-follow-up-email, email-only, bulletin note,
   short website statement, or silence. Default for a pastoral moment: the pulpit carries the
   word; a short plain email follows for those who weren't there; social media usually stays quiet
   or reposts the email. Reverse only for reasons.
5. **Set length.** Two to three short paragraphs is right for most public-moment pastoral letters;
   five is almost always too many. If the clergy wants more, or the letter runs over 300 words,
   ask what a member reading this on a phone at 6:45 a.m. needs and cut the rest.
6. **Draft, then let the clergy own it.** Draft in the clergy's honest voice (see "Voice Fidelity
   When Drafting For Clergy" below), not HR or comms boilerplate. The clergy is the author of
   record and must be able to read the message aloud without wincing.

## Drafting a Congregational Death Notice — Numbered Checklist

The single most consequential pastoral communication a clergy person writes. Run this every time.

1. **Confirm family consent and preferred wording before writing anything public.** Ask what they
   want said, what may be shared about circumstances (especially in a suicide, overdose, or violent
   death), how the deceased is to be named (given name, Hebrew name, kunya, honorifics), and
   whether the announcement should precede or follow their own notifications to extended family.
   Nothing goes to the congregation before the family gives the wording.
2. **Draft in the pastoral voice, first person, from the clergy.** "It is with deep sadness that I
   share with you the death of our friend and member [Name], who died [when] [surrounded by family
   / peacefully / after a long illness — only what the family authorized]." Do not open with
   logistics.
3. **Name the tradition's practice.** Christian: date and time of the visitation, funeral or
   memorial service, committal, and reception. Jewish: the funeral (often within 24-72 hours),
   shiva schedule and location, and any restrictions (no flowers; make a donation in memory if the
   family requests). Muslim: the janazah prayer time and location, burial arrangements, and how
   the community can support the family during the mourning period. Do not import another
   tradition's cadence.
4. **Include a specific way to help.** Meal train link, shiva minyan sign-up, hospitality for
   out-of-town family, transportation, childcare — whatever the family has authorized. "Let us
   know if you need anything" places the burden on the grieving family; a named coordinator with
   an email address does not.
5. **Close with a pastoral sentence, not an org sign-off.** "Please hold the [Family Name] family
   in your prayers this week" is pastoral; "For more information, contact the office" may also
   appear, but the pastoral sentence comes last.
6. **Sign with the clergy's own name and role.** "Rabbi [Name]", "Pastor [Name]", "Imam [Name]",
   not "The Communications Team" or "[Org]".
7. **Channel discipline.** Congregational email is the default. No social media post until the
   family has said publicly or given explicit permission — assume extended family and colleagues
   learn from social feeds. Never post a photograph of the deceased the family has not selected.
8. **Follow-up cadence.** A week after the funeral, a short second note from clergy — inviting
   continued support of the family, naming any scheduled memorial (shloshim, forty-day memorial,
   month's mind, one-year yahrzeit or unveiling) — signals the community keeps mourning after the
   crowd goes home.

## Drafting a Crisis / Tragedy Response — Numbered Checklist

1. **Speed vs. accuracy.** Within hours for a local event that directly touched the community
   (the shooting was at our school, the fire was in our building); within a day for a regional
   event with likely direct connections (many members may have been at the concert, may know
   victims); within two to three days, or not at all, for distant national moments that don't
   uniquely require our voice. Being first is not being pastoral.
2. **Confirm facts you will state.** Number of dead, names, cause — only what authorities have
   publicly confirmed. Letters that get facts wrong lose the community's trust for years. "We do
   not yet know" is a legitimate pastoral sentence.
3. **Lament first, meaning second, action last.** Name the pain honestly, then any meaning-making
   the tradition offers, and only last any action (prayer service, blood drive, donation link).
   Action first, lament as an afterthought, reads as institutional.
4. **Do not proof-text.** Follow the citation rule under "Language and Register Discipline"; a
   misapplied text lands worse than no text.
5. **Name the tradition's practice without moralizing at outsiders.** "As we prepare our hearts
   for Yom Kippur this week, this news lands especially heavily" is invitation; "you should be
   preparing for Yom Kippur" is scolding. The message is legible to members and non-members alike.
6. **Prophetic without partisan.** Name the moral wrong (gun violence, hate, dehumanization)
   without naming a party, candidate, or election. If it cannot be named without partisan
   reference, route through counsel and `nonprofit-faith-church-governance` first.
7. **Keep to the length rule** (checklist step 5) and publish under the clergy's name. Consider
   whether a short in-person gathering (vigil, prayer service, open sanctuary hour) is the right
   pastoral response and name it in the letter.

## Liturgical / Annual Calendar Cadence

Peg the cadence to the constituents' traditions, not a generic calendar. Describe the practice, do
not endorse it, and never assume every reader observes.

- **Christian congregation cadence (Western)**: Advent letter (four Sundays before Christmas,
  usually the first Sunday of Advent), Christmas Eve/Day greeting, Ash Wednesday call to Lent,
  Holy Week note (usually Palm Sunday), Easter greeting, Pentecost, and a Christ the King or
  program-year-close note in late November. Ordinary-time weeks carry the weekly sermon-adjacent
  email but no seasonal letter. Eastern Orthodox congregations follow the Julian calendar for
  Pascha in many US jurisdictions — check the specific parish before pinning a date.
- **Jewish congregation cadence**: monthly Rosh Chodesh notes for those who mark them; a High
  Holy Days letter in Elul (roughly a month before Rosh Hashanah) covering shofar service,
  selichot, Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur, and ticketing/security; Sukkot/Simchat Torah note;
  Hanukkah greeting; Purim/megillah reading; Passover (letter with seder resources, kashering
  information, sale of chametz); Yom HaShoah and Yom HaAtzmaut (with denominational variation);
  Shavuot; Tisha B'Av. The weekly Shabbat email is standard and typically goes out Thursday, not
  Friday, so members receive it before the start of Shabbat.
- **Muslim community cadence**: monthly community notes; a Ramadan letter shortly before the
  month begins with iftar schedule, taraweeh, community expectations around fasting, and a
  reminder for those who cannot fast; Eid al-Fitr greeting on the confirmed date; Hajj/Eid
  al-Adha letter; Muharram/Ashura note where the community observes; Mawlid where observed.
  Ramadan communications assume some members fast and some do not (pregnancy, illness, travel,
  age, menstruation) and never shame non-fasters.
- **Faith-based social service org cadence**: an interfaith org (federation, hospital, refugee
  agency, food bank with religious identity) pegs its cadence to the communities it serves. A
  Jewish federation sends High Holy Days and Passover messages to donors and staff. A Catholic
  hospital marks Ash Wednesday, Holy Week, and Easter for staff and volunteers. A multi-faith
  social service org typically marks the three or four moments where a greeting from the ED lands
  (Ramadan/Eid, High Holy Days, Christmas/Easter, and one civic moment like Thanksgiving or MLK
  Day) — trying to greet everything greets nothing well.

## Artifact-Specific Guidance

- **Illness / hospitalization notice**: only what the person or family authorized, in writing.
  Never publish a diagnosis, prognosis, or hospital location without explicit permission. "Please
  hold [Name] and her family in prayer this week; she has asked for privacy about details" is
  more pastoral than a specific medical bulletin.
- **Wedding / bar-bat mitzvah / baptism / aqiqah announcement**: the tradition's vocabulary;
  consent from the family or couple before including names, dates, or photos; short.
- **Clergy misconduct disclosure to the congregation**: draft only in coordination with counsel,
  the insurer (many policies have notification and communication requirements), denominational
  judicatory, and — separately — the media plan owned by `nonprofit-media-relations`, but not
  written by them. The letter names what happened (only what has been established or authorized
  to disclose), acknowledges harm before process, states steps underway (investigation, reporting
  to authorities, cooperation with the denomination), does not minimize or sensationalize, does not
  wait for legal clarity to acknowledge harm, and does not name the accused if the investigation
  is not concluded and disclosure is not authorized. It gives anyone with additional information a
  way to reach an independent investigator directly, not through the org. It closes with care for
  those most harmed, not by defending the institution. Written under senior clergy's name, not
  the communications team. Route reporting-obligation questions to
  `nonprofit-faith-employment-ministerial-exception`.
- **Denominational statement / pastoral letter**: after the denomination's governance process has
  adopted a position, the bishop, presiding elder, general minister, or head of communion carries
  the decision to the membership in the voice of the tradition ("Our tradition teaches…", "The
  Book of Order requires…", "The general convention has adopted…"), not of a partisan.
  Distinguish (a) matters the denomination teaches, (b) matters on which it has taken a public
  position, and (c) matters left to individual conscience. Address it when the tradition has
  spoken; avoid it when the tradition has not; teach on it when members are asking and the
  tradition has resources but no binding position.
- **Farewell letter (outgoing clergy)**: short, gracious, no unfinished business made public, no
  parting shots, no naming of individuals in disputes, gratitude specific enough to be real. Names
  the ending, not the community's future direction.
- **Arrival letter (incoming clergy)**: introduces self as pastor rather than as a resume, names
  the transition, honors the outgoing clergy, does not preview policy changes, invites first
  meetings. Both letters are coordinated with the search committee, denominational transition
  officer, and (where applicable) `nonprofit-faith-church-governance`.
- **Sermon-adjacent weekly congregation email**: a paragraph or two of pastoral framing plus that
  week's calendar. Not a full sermon recap, marketing newsletter, or fundraising ask (those live
  in `nonprofit-faith-stewardship-giving`).
- **Prayer request / prayer-chain content**: see Confidentiality below. Common discipline: the
  chain gets a first name and one sentence ("Sarah, for healing"), not a diagnosis; the clergy
  holds anything more privately. Restate the confidentiality rule at the top of every
  distribution so new members learn it.
- **Building milestone (dedication, cornerstone, mortgage-burning, deconsecration, closure)**:
  marks meaning, not construction status. A deconsecration or closure letter is an act of grief
  and gratitude — draft it with the same care as a funeral, because for many members it is one.

## Language and Register Discipline

- **Direct address that includes without excluding.** "Friends", "beloved community" or "members
  and friends of [congregation]" reads to members without pushing out a first-time reader.
  "Fellow believers" or "brethren" is denomination-specific and may exclude even members who
  don't use that vocabulary; use only when it is genuinely the community's word.
- **Scripture / sacred text citation.** Cite chapter and verse or equivalent locator (Isaiah 40:1;
  Sura al-Baqara 2:286; Pirkei Avot 2:16). One well-chosen passage does more than three unrelated
  ones. Do not proof-text — readers who know the text hear a verse attached to a point it does
  not make.
- **Multi-faith staff and beneficiary contexts.** A Christian ED writing to an interfaith staff
  names her own tradition without imposing it ("This is Holy Week for those of us in the
  Christian tradition; I know some of you are observing Passover this week, and I wish you a
  meaningful chag"). A Jewish federation writing to a broader donor community names its Jewish
  identity clearly without assuming every reader is Jewish. A Muslim org's message to non-Muslim
  partners at Eid can explain the practice briefly without proselytizing.
- **Lament without sentimentality.** Pastoral communications are among the few professional
  communications where lament is not only permitted but expected. Name the pain in one honest
  sentence and stop — no accessorizing ("this heartbreaking, devastating, crushing news"). One true
  sentence lands; three decorated ones read as performance.
- **Length.** If a public-moment pastoral letter runs over 300 words, ask what can go (see
  checklist step 5).

## Channels and Pacing

- **The pulpit is the primary channel** for a moment the community is already gathering to
  process; a short plain email follows for those who weren't there.
- **Congregational email** carries most between-service pastoral communication. Send Thursday
  (not Friday) for Jewish communities so it lands before Shabbat. Send well before Maghrib during
  Ramadan so it lands before iftar. Send hard news early morning rather than late evening, so
  readers have the day to absorb it, not the night.
- **Bulletin / order of service** carries dated logistical detail (funeral times, service
  schedules) the pastoral email can reference.
- **Website statement** fits a clergy misconduct disclosure or formal denominational statement that
  will be linked to for months; keep it short, signed, and dated.
- **Social media** is usually the wrong channel for a pastoral moment; reposting the email or
  website statement is fine, but writing pastoral content into a platform post (e.g., a 280-character post) strips the
  register. Stay off social media unless the family or moment specifically calls for it. For
  a death, default to no social post unless the family requests it.
- **Silence is a channel too** — a decision, not a default.

## Confidentiality and Privilege

Get these wrong once and it takes years to rebuild trust.

- **Clergy-penitent privilege** protects confidential communications made to clergy in the
  clergy's spiritual capacity — but scope varies by state, and many states have narrowed or
  abolished privilege for disclosures of child abuse or neglect (mandatory-reporter statutes). Do
  not draft any message that references or alludes to a confidential disclosure until
  `nonprofit-faith-employment-ministerial-exception` has resolved the reporting analysis.
- **Prayer-chain confidentiality**: publish only what the individual or family authorized for the
  chain, distinguished from what clergy hold in pastoral confidence, and remind the chain in
  writing at every distribution not to forward outside it. Assume a chain leaks; write accordingly.
- **Hospital visitation privacy**: HIPAA does not directly bind clergy visits in most cases, but
  a hospital's clergy-visit list is HIPAA-governed and the hospital cannot share a patient's
  presence without patient consent. Do not name the hospital, unit, or diagnosis in writing
  without explicit family consent.
- **When a family gives permission to share**: get it in writing (a text or email is fine) and
  note the specific wording authorized. Do not extrapolate — permission to announce a death is not
  permission to disclose cause. Default to less rather than more.

## Voice Fidelity When Drafting For Clergy

Most pastoral letters land or fail on whether they sound like the clergy actually talking.

1. **Interview the clergy for three or four phrases they would say aloud** about the moment,
   before drafting — what they are actually thinking, not what they think they should say.
2. **Build the draft around those phrases.** No comms boilerplate ("Our thoughts and prayers are
   with…"), no language the clergy would never use in the pulpit, no smoothing away their honest
   register.
3. **Match the clergy's cadence** — long sentences with subordinate clauses or short declarative
   ones. A draft that flattens either loses the voice.
4. **Leave the hard edges.** If the clergy is angry, uncertain, or grieving, the draft carries
   that — controlled but not sanitized. A comms-scrubbed letter reads as institutional even when
   the words are right.
5. **The clergy is the author of record.** Invite them to change any sentence, and treat every
   edit as improving voice fidelity, not degrading polish. Publish under their own name. If they
   have not finished processing a hard moment, draft short and honest — "I am still holding this"
   is a legitimate pastoral sentence.

Crisis-letter skeleton (fill only with confirmed facts and the clergy's own phrases):

```
Subject: [Plain, specific: "After last night in [place]"]

Dear [community's own form of address],

[Lament: one honest sentence naming what happened, using only facts confirmed by
authorities. "We do not yet know ___" if details are unconfirmed.]

[Meaning: one or two sentences from the tradition, in the clergy's words; at most one
cited text with its locator.]

[Action: the gathering or help being offered — what, when, where, who to contact
by name.]

[Pastoral closing sentence.]

[Clergy name], [role]
```

## Verify Before Acting

- [ ] Every fact stated (names, numbers, cause, times) is publicly confirmed or authorized.
- [ ] Family wording and permissions for any death, illness or milestone are recorded in
      writing, and the draft discloses nothing beyond them.
- [ ] Public-moment letters are within the length rule (about 300 words or less).
- [ ] No candidate, party or election is named; near an election, counsel has seen named-issue text.
- [ ] Any reference to a confidential disclosure has been cleared through the reporting analysis.
- [ ] Holy-day dates and send times were checked for the specific community.
- [ ] The clergy has reviewed and adopted the draft, and it is signed with their name.

## Common Failure Modes

Each is covered in the section named; use this as a final scan.

- **Wrong register** — brand voice ("At [Org] we believe…") when a pastoral word was needed, or a
  pastoral message when a clear executive statement about a decision was needed (Core Frameworks).
- **Saying something on everything / silence when a word was expected** (decision checklist step 2).
- **Prophetic voice slipping into partisan endorsement** — jeopardizes 501(c)(3) status (step 3).
- **Comms-scrubbed pastoral letters** (Voice Fidelity).
- **Proof-texting scripture** (Language and Register Discipline).
- **Announcing what the family did not authorize** — cause, location, or detail (death-notice
  step 1; Confidentiality).
- **Clergy-misconduct letter that reads as institutional defense** (Artifact-Specific Guidance).
- **Wrong channel** — a death post on the church Instagram before the family has notified extended
  family (Channels and Pacing).
- **Too long** (decision checklist step 5).
- **Denominational and congregational voices out of sync** (Core Frameworks).

## For advisors

- Your job is voice fidelity, not authorship: draft short, publish under the clergy's name, and
  resist smoothing every hard edge.
- Coordinate with, but do not defer to, marketing, legal and media staff; they own their
  artifacts, and you own the letter that must land in a different register.
- In a misconduct crisis, insist the pastoral letter be drafted alongside — not by — the press
  response, so the congregation is not addressed in the register of a legal statement.
